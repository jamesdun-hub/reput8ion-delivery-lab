There is an exercise we run near the start of most clarity engagements. Each member of the leadership team, separately, answers one question: what does this organisation stand for?

We have run it dozens of times. The record is eleven materially different answers from a team of eleven. The typical result is five from seven. Nobody is being difficult. Each answer is sincere, considered and defensible. They are just different.

The interesting part is what happens next. The first instinct in the room is always to treat the divergence as a communications failure. Someone suggests a messaging house. Someone else suggests the answers are really the same thing said differently, which is comforting and almost never true. Read them side by side and they describe different companies with different priorities, different appetites for risk and different views of who the customer is.

That divergence is not a wording problem. It is the strategy showing through. A leadership team that has never been forced to argue its way to one answer will produce five, and every stakeholder who meets more than one of them will notice before the team does. Journalists notice fastest of all, because finding the gap between two executives is roughly half the job.

The fix is not a document. It is a disagreement, had properly, once, in a room where it is allowed to be a disagreement. What the organisation stands for is a decision, and like most decisions it is avoided for as long as avoiding it stays cheap. Our job is usually to make the avoidance visible, then hold the room while the argument happens.

The document comes afterwards, and by then it almost writes itself, because it is describing something that now exists.

If you suspect your own team would fail the exercise, it costs one email to find out.
